01

IDENTITY

What are Archaea?

Archaea are one of the major domains of cellular life. They are prokaryotic in cellular organization but are evolutionarily distinct from Bacteria. Many information-processing systems in Archaea, including parts of transcription and replication, share deep similarities with their eukaryotic counterparts.

Keep

Archaea are globally distributed, metabolically diverse, and central to major biogeochemical cycles.

Discard

“All archaea are extremophiles.” Many thrive in ordinary marine, soil, sediment, host-associated, and engineered environments.

Taxonomy caution: archaeal classification changes as genomes and phylogenomic methods improve. Always record the taxonomy source and release date.

02

CELL BIOLOGY

Membranes, walls, and information systems

  • Membranes: archaeal lipids typically use ether-linked isoprenoid chains attached to glycerol-1-phosphate. Some lineages form membrane-spanning tetraether lipids.
  • Cell envelopes: many archaea use proteinaceous S-layers. Archaeal cell walls do not contain bacterial peptidoglycan, although some methanogens produce pseudomurein.
  • Gene expression: archaeal transcription machinery contains components related to eukaryotic systems, while translation retains both broadly conserved and domain-specific features.
  • Cell division: mechanisms vary across lineages and include ESCRT-related systems, FtsZ-based systems, and less fully characterized alternatives.
03

METABOLISM

Energy metabolisms with planetary consequences

Methanogenesis

Biological methane production is restricted to Archaea. Pathways use methyl-coenzyme M reductase and can draw on carbon dioxide, acetate, methylated compounds, or other substrates depending on the lineage.

Anaerobic methane oxidation

Several archaeal groups oxidize methane without oxygen, often in syntrophic association with bacterial partners and with electron acceptors linked to surrounding geochemistry.

Ammonia oxidation

Ammonia-oxidizing archaea are abundant in marine and terrestrial systems and contribute substantially to nitrification, often under low-ammonia conditions.

Carbon fixation and heterotrophy

Archaea use multiple carbon fixation routes and diverse heterotrophic strategies. A genome annotation alone may not establish in situ activity.

Light-driven energy capture

Some archaea use retinal-based rhodopsins to generate ion gradients. This differs from chlorophyll-based oxygenic photosynthesis.

Sulfur and hydrogen

Many lineages use sulfur compounds or molecular hydrogen in energy metabolism, especially in anoxic and high-temperature systems.

04

ECOLOGY

Habitats and interactions

Archaea occur from sunlit oceans to deep sediments, soils, hot springs, hypersaline systems, subsurface habitats, animal microbiomes, and wastewater reactors. Environmental interpretation requires more than detecting a marker gene.

  1. 1Confirm the sampling environment, depth, chemistry, and collection method.
  2. 2Distinguish relative abundance from absolute abundance and activity.
  3. 3Separate genomic potential from transcript, protein, metabolite, or rate evidence.
  4. 4Consider partners, viruses, and spatial organization.
  5. 5Record whether a conclusion comes from isolates, enrichments, genomes, or community data.

06

AI USES

Where AI can help—and where it can fail

Useful roles

  • Query expansion and literature triage
  • Structured extraction from defined sources
  • Annotation prioritization and anomaly review
  • Code drafting and documentation
  • Hypothesis organization

High-risk failure modes

  • Invented citations or identifiers
  • Outdated taxonomy
  • Overconfident gene-function assignment
  • Loss of environmental context
  • Causal claims from correlations

Minimum rule: no scientific claim leaves the workflow without a source identifier, supporting evidence, uncertainty note, and named human verification step.

GLOSSARY

Starter glossary

ANME
Anaerobic methanotrophic archaea.
MAG
Metagenome-assembled genome.
mcrA
A commonly used marker for methyl-coenzyme M reductase, central to methanogenesis and related methane metabolism.
S-layer
A surface layer made from protein or glycoprotein subunits.
Syntrophy
A metabolic partnership in which organisms depend on exchanged products or coupled reactions.
Tetraether lipid
A membrane-spanning archaeal lipid common in several lineages and environments.
